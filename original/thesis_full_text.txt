Abstract

CO2 ocean sequestration provides a promising alternative for reducing greenhouse gas
emissions provided an economic process is developed. The need to find a low cost
process for long term CO2 sequestration is the drive behind this study of the direct ocean
release of CO2/N2 mixtures. Emitted flue gases are usually at most 15% CO2 with the
remainder consisting primarily of N2. Processes that involve the direct release of pure
CO2 first require capture and separation of CO2 from those flue gases. The direct release
of low purity CO2/N2 mixture eliminates the costly step of separation. Furthermore, N2
discourages the formation of hydrate, leading to greater dissolution rates at injection
depths below 500 m.

This report discusses existing models of both pure CO2 release and the P-GLAD model
for low purity CO2 injection developed by Saito et al. (2001). From the existing pure
CO2 direct release models a numeric model for direct mixture release is developed. The
direct release of 15%, 50%, 85% and 100% from injection depths of 300, 500, 1000, and
1500 meters is simulated.

Finally the simulation results are used to compare the estimated cost of mixed gas direct
release to that of the P-GLAD and pure CO2 release. It is estimated that mixed gas direct
release costs 118$/ton CO2. This is less than the estimate cost of liquid CO2 release
(126$/ton CO2), but double the estimated cost of the P-GLAD process ($68/ton CO2).

1. Introduction

The energy usage of our growing worldwide population has resulted rising levels of
greenhouse gas emissions. Over the past 150 years, atmospheric CO2 concentrations have
risen from 280 ppm to 370 ppm as a result of fossil fuel usage. It is estimated that CO2
worldwide emissions in 1990 were greater than 20 Gt of CO2 per year (DOE/ER-30194,
1994). It is possible that atmospheric CO2 emissions will have to be limited if we are to
continue to meet the energy demands of a growing population without contributing
further to ecological decline. Current CO2 sequestration research examines alternative
storage locations for waste CO2 and may provide cost-effective processes for CO2
emissions reduction.

Geological formations provide opportunities for long term CO2 storage. CO2 can be
dissolved into the salt water of deep aquifers. Additionally, oil and gas reservoirs provide
capped porous rock formations for CO2 storage accompanied by the possibility of
enhanced oil recovery. Coal bed CO2 injection provides similar benefits, with long term
CO2 storage accompanied by enhanced methane recovery.

In addition to geological CO2 sequestration, current research is examining ways to take
advantage of the ocean's large natural CO2 storage capabilities. The DOE reports that the
ocean occupies more than 70% of the Earth's surface, giving it huge potential for CO2
storage (Golomb). Additionally, a major portion of atmospheric CO2 eventually ends up
in the ocean through natural processes and the quantity of CO2 to be stored in the ocean is
small relative to the quantity already there. It is estimated by the DOE that the ocean
would show a 0.016% increase in CO2 after disposal of all current annual carbon
emissions (Golomb 1994). Proposed CO2 ocean sequestration techniques can be divided
into shallow water, midwater and deepwater releases. The CO2 transportation and

                                                                                                                                16
dissolution processes of each differ due to oceanic conditions and resulting CO2 phase
behavior.

Whether geological or oceanic, nearly all current sequestration research explores storage
of pure CO2 gas. However, waste CO2 gas is most frequently emitted in gas mixtures and
the application of any such processes requires the separation of CO2. Gas separation is a
costly procedure, with cost estimates of one current chemical absorption method ranging
from $33 to $43 per ton of CO2 (Saito et al. 2001). Such costs make examining mixed
gas sequestration, which explores the use of natural processes to eliminate the separation
step, worthwhile. Such work is not only applicable to gas sequestration, but could also
have widespread implications to all gas separation.

Recently, a team of Japanese researchers proposed the P-GLAD system for low purity
CO2 ocean disposal (Saito et al. 2001). The P-GLAD system consists of an inverse J
pipeline and takes advantage of the gas lift effect to allow for shallow water injection of
low purity gaseous CO2 and deepwater release of dissolved CO2. The system eliminates
the costly steps of gas separation and CO2 liquefacation yet provides the environmental
advantage of deepwater disposal. The experimental and numerical work of Saito et al
,(2001) demonstrates the economic advantage the P-GLAD system holds over direct
liquid CO2 release.

This report explores further mixed gas ocean release. It numerically examines direct
ocean release of CO2 and N2 gas mixtures. It then compares these results to those found
for the P-GLAD system. Chapter 2 provides background information on phase behavior
and its role in ocean CO2 sequestration. Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 provide a detailed
discussion of the P-GLAD and open water bubble release models, respectively. They
include discussions of the governing equations and assumptions of each model. Finally,
Chapter 5 presents and discusses the simulation results and Chapter 6 highlights the
conclusions of this study.

2. Chemistry of Ocean Sequestration

Ocean sequestration involves complex systems that build on the basic concepts of phase
behavior, multiphase flow, and mass transfer. This chapter begins with a discussion of
hydrates and their impact on CO2 sequestration. It then covers the basic phase behavior
of CO2, N2, and CO2/ N2 mixtures. Finally, it shows the application of phase behavior in
proposed CO2 ocean sequestration technology.

2.1. Phase Behavior

Thermodynamics governs the properties of chemical components at varying pressures and
temperatures. The temperatures and pressures of oceans, and the variations of those
conditions with depth, makes phase behavior a key component to ocean sequestration. In
the case of hydrocarbon ocean sequestration, low temperatures and high pressure induce
the formation of hydrates. Hydrate formation must be accounted for in ocean
sequestration models, as hydrates posses unique properties and alter mass transfer and
bubble transport processes.

2.1.1 Hydrates
Hydrates are crystalline structures that form when hydrocarbons are in water at low
temperatures and high pressures. Water molecules form a cage like structure which
encases the hydrocarbon molecule. Much about hydrate behavior remains debated. For
example, there is a great deal of uncertainty surrounding the exact formation,
dissociation, and dissolution processes of hydrates. However, the importance of hydrate
formation in CO2 ocean sequestration has been recognized and has spurred a great deal of
research.

In an early study on CO2 dissolution behavior, Aya et al. (1992) report that the
dissolution rate through a CO2 hydrate coated bubble is lower than that through a bubble
without hydrate. This idea is consistently supported throughout the literature. Figure 2-1

Brewer et al. (2000) points out that the formation of hydrates can have an important
impact on the success of a sequestration project. Hydrate formation can benefit
deepwater sequestration by increased sequestration time resulting from reduced CO2
transfer rates. However, hydrate formation at shallower depths can result in ineffective
sequestration if CO2 is slowed enough that droplets reach the mixed zone without total
dissolution. Failure to include and properly account for hydrate formation in a
sequestration project can result in enormous economic costs.

2.1.3 Carbon Dioxide and Nitrogen Mixture Phase Behavior
The phase behavior of gaseous mixtures lies between the extremes of the pure
components of the mixture. Nitrogen as a pure element is lighter and harder to compress
than carbon dioxide. Furthermore, nitrogen hydrates are much harder to form than carbon
dioxide hydrates. When nitrogen is added to carbon dioxide hydrate formation pressure
at any given temperature increases. As a result, nitrogen and carbon dioxide mixtures
have smaller hydrate zones than pure carbon dioxide.

Figure 2-5 compares predicted hydrate formation pressures for different nitrogen and
carbon dioxide mixtures as predicted using the CSMHYD thermodynamic program

                                                                                                                                23
(Sloan, 1998). For a CO2 content below about 50% mixture hydrate formation pressure
drastically increases. As the mixture formation pressure increases for a given
temperature, the hydrate formation zone decreases. Pure CO2 will form hydrate at depths
greater than 500 m at ocean pressures of approximately 50 atms and higher and ocean
temperatures below about 279 K. In contrast, a 50/50 mixture of CO2/N2 at 50 atms
requires a temperature of 277 K or less to form hydrate.

2.2. Ocean Sequestration Processes

In shallow waters of 200-400 m, gaseous CO2 is directly released through a pipe. The
density of the CO2 is less than that of the seawater and the gas bubbles rise. During the
bubble ascent, CO2 dissolves into the surrounding seawater. The upper mixed layer of
the ocean extends approximately 100 m below the surface (Liro et al. 1992). To limit
CO2 release to the atmosphere through the ocean surface, any CO2 released should be
dissolved before reaching the upper mixed layer. Implementation of shallow water
injection would presumably occur at ocean side power plants. Captured flue gases would
be sent through a gas separation process. The extracted CO2 could then be injected into
the seawater through a gas pipeline. Shallow water injection offers the most economic
capital, energy, and transportation costs, but its long term CO2 storage capabilities are
open to question.

Midwater release has been proposed for locations offshore in ocean depths of 400-1800
m. In midwater release processes, flue gases are captured, separated, and compressed.
Liquefied CO2 is then transported to the intended dumping site by ship and deposited at
the desired depth through a transportation pipe. As discussed in the previous section,
temperature and pressure conditions at depths greater than 400 m are such that CO2 forms
hydrates. Thus, the injected bubbles form an outer layer of hydrates and CO2 dissolution
occurs as the hydrate coated bubbles rise. Midwater release provides greater security for
long term CO2 storage. However, the compression and transportation of midwater release
are costly. Saito et al. (2001) estimate the total cost of direct release of liquid CO2 at
depths near 1000m at $118 per a ton of CO2.

In addition to mid and shallow water release, storage of liquid CO2 on the sea floor has
been proposed. This is achieved by injection of liquid CO2, after capture, separation and
compression, at depths greater than 1800-2700 m depending on temperature profile. At
such depths the density of liquid CO2 is greater than that of seawater. Thus, injected CO2
sinks to the sea floor. Deepwater injection, like midwater injection, is promising for long

                                                                                                                                25
term CO2 storage. However, it is accompanied by even greater transportation and
compressions costs than midwater release.
Considerable research and modeling of CO2 ocean sequestration have recently been
conducted and provide insight into the processes of CO2 transportation and dissolution in
the ocean. However, implementation of proposed ocean sequestration techniques has
been limited, in part, by economic restraints. The need for an economic long term CO2
sequestration procedure is the driving force behind the models discussed in the next two
chapters. The P-GLAD system, proposed by Saito et al.(2000), limits compression and
separation costs, but has higher capital and transportation costs than other methods.
Mixed gas direct release limits separation costs, but may require greater compression
costs. The aim of this study is, in part, to provide an economic comparison of the models
as well as a preliminary assessment of the economics.

Saito et al. (2000) propose a gas-lift J-tube system for the storage of low purity CO2
mixtures. The system allows for shallow water injection of low purity CO2, eliminating
the cost of gas compression that accompanies deep-water injection. After injection, CO2

indissoluble gases are emitted from the system upon reaching the top of the riser. The

and density differential effects. The Gas Lift Advanced Dissolution system proposed by
Saito et al. (2000) provides a comparatively low cost method for CO2 sequestration by
eliminating the necessity of pre-injection separation and gas compression. Additionally,
the system provides the long term security of deepwater deposition and CO2 storage.

3.3 Simplified Model

The model used by Saito et al. (2001) provides accurate, detailed results, including
unsteady flow but at the significant cost of complexity. This study examines a simpler
model that begins by separating the J-tube into two sections, the upriser and the
downriser. Flow in the upriser is calculated using concepts of multiphase flow in a pipe.
Flow in the downriser is a simpler single phase flow problem controlled by the
conservation of mass.

The initial conditions of the J-tube model differ from typical well applications in that the
flow rate of water is not specified. The rate of injection of gas is controlled but the
amount of seawater is not pumped into the system at a controlled rate. Instead, the flow
of water is determined by the boundary pressures at the pipe openings and the amount of
water the injected gas is capable of lifting. The model upriser is 200 meters long with a
boundary at 300 meters below the surface. The downriser is 900 meters long with a
boundary 1000 meters below the surface.

3.3.4 Solving the Model
The pressure gradient equations and the initial conditions are used to solve for the J-
tube's pumping ability, captured by the fluid velocity variable. A guess and check
procedure is used to find the fluid velocity that correctly solves the pressure gradient
equations, setting the pressures at the bottom of the upriser and the bottom of the
downriser to correct hydrostatic pressures.

The 200 meter upriser is divided into 5 meter sections. Fluid properties are determined
for each section and used along with the gas injection rate and the guessed flow rate of
seawater to solve the pressure gradient equation in the upriser. Conservation of mass
requires that all the water flowing from the upriser flows into the downriser. Thus, the
velocity of the water at the top of the downriser must equal the velocity at the top of the
upriser. This velocity is used to solve the pressure gradient in the downriser, which is
assumed to be one long pipe with constant fluid properties. The predicted pressure at the
bottom of the downriser is then checked against the actual hydrostatic pressure. If the
two do not match, a new water flow rate is guessed and the procedure is repeated.

4. Single Bubble Release

The single bubble release model examines the transportation and dissolution processes
that occur with the release of a single bubble of gas and various depths. The model
predicts the velocity, bubble diameter and CO2 concentration over time. Several studies
have numerically modeled the release of pure CO2 bubble into the ocean. Mori and
Muchizuki (1998) examined the behavior of pure CO2 bubbles in seawater with the
intention of better understanding the effect of hydrate formation on CO2 dissolution rates.
Hirai et al. (1999) also provide a model of pure CO2 direct ocean release in their study on
the environmental impacts of CO2 ocean sequestration. The past models of pure CO2
release provide a basis upon which to build a model of mixed gas bubble release.

Bubble release models are composed of two processes, gas dissolution and bubble
transport. To accurately model bubble release the governing equations of both processes
and the procedure to simultaneously solving those equations must be understood. Also
essential to solving the model is correct evaluation of its parameters and properties.

4.1 Governing Equations

4.1.1 Bubble Transportation
Bubble rise velocity is dependent on numerous factors, including bubble shape, density,
and size as well as the properties of the surroundings through which it travels. In the
case of ocean gas injection it is reasonable to assume spherical shapes with negligible
deformation. Yabe et al. (1999) examine the validity of this assumption by examining
the Weber number, the dimensionless number directly related to bubble deformation.
They found a maximum Weber number in their experiments, 0.17, below the Weber
number, 0.5, indicative of bubble deformation. Additionally, the numeric models of Mori
and Mochizuki (1998) for rigid spheres more closely match experimental data than those
for fluid spheres.

The right hand side of equation 4-5 multiplies the bubble volume by bubble density

will face changing thermodynamic conditions that lead to both changing bubble and

Mori and Mochizuki (1998) solve equation 4-1 for the rate of change of diameter with

over time must be calculated. Because volume is not conserved, diameter change is not
directly representative of the rate of mass loss but is rather a result of the combined
thermodynamic changes and gas dissolution. Therefore, determining the change in
bubble concentration requires the change in mass.

This model calculates the change in mass in a given time step. It is assumed that the
solubility of N2 in seawater is negligible, and thus the entire quantity of mass lost is CO2.
This assumption is reasonable as the solubility of nitrogen in seawater is magnitudes of
order below that of carbon dioxide. The entire quantity of mass lost is easily converted to
a molar quantity, from which the change in composition is calculated. Using the
predicted density and bubble composition at the next time step the bubble diameter is
predicted.

4.3.3.1 REPORTED MASS TRANSFER COEFFICIENTS
A good deal of discrepancy surrounds the rate of mass dissolution of CO2 into seawater.
Furthermore, methods of measuring and reporting the CO2 mass transfer coefficient are
inconsistent. Using reported mass transfer coefficients requires attention to the details of
their source.

Mori and Mochizuki (1998) estimate the value of the mass transfer coefficient using
empirical correlations and a modified Wilke-Chang equation to determine the coefficient
of diffusion of CO2 in water. The correlations define the mass transfer rate as a function
of bubble diameter and rise velocity and seawater viscosity and density. The result is a
mass transfer rate that varies over time and is strongly dependent on bubble velocity. The
study examines diameter change over time, and thus the coefficient is reported in units of
m/s.

Experimental work suggests a constant mass transfer coefficient. The constant
coefficient of 2 x 10-4 m/s used in the P-GLAD simulation by Saito et al. (2000) is one of
the higher determined mass transfer coefficients and does not include hydrate formation.
Hirai et al. (1996) report a mass transfer rate of 1.25 x 10-5 kg m-2 s-1 for non-hydrate
coated bubbles.

A greater amount of work deals with the mass transfer process of hydrate coated bubbles.
Brewer et al. (2000) provide a mass transfer coefficient on the lower end of the scale,
based on sea experiments, of 3.4 x 10-6 mol m-2 s-1 for hydrate coated bubbles. Two
separate reports, Fujioka et al. (1994) and Aya et al. (1992), report bubble diameter
reduction rates of 5.0 x 10-7 and 3.9 x 10-9 m/s, respectively. Tabe et al. (1999) report a
constant mass transfer rate of CO2 through hydrate of 1.15 x 10-4 kg m-2 s-1. Hirai et al.
(1996) report varying mass transfer rates through hydrate depending on temperature,
pressure, and rise velocity. Their experimentally measured rates range from

                                                                                                                                44
approximately 3 x 10-4 to 1.5 x 10-3 kg m-2 s-1, but they offer no correlations for
calculating the rates.

injected gas. However, as N2 increases the mass transfer rate should decrease. In this

5.3 Economic Comparison and Analysis

5.3.1 Cost Factors
Predicting the cost of mixed gas sequestration is difficult due to a lack of available
information regarding such processes. The aim of mixed gas injection is to reduce costs
by eliminating the necessity of CO2 separation. Additionally, the hydrate formation zone
is decreased by impurities resulting in a range of depths over which lower purity bubbles
will exhibit faster dissolution than pure CO2 bubbles. However, this range of depths is
not directly equal to the difference in the hydrate zones, as the mass transfer coefficient is
expected to decrease with decreasing concentration of CO2 in the bubble.

                                                                                                                                78
The benefits of injecting low purity CO2 must be weighed against the costs. Increased
impurity and injection depth lead to increased compression costs. Additionally, higher
levels of impurities mean higher volumes of gas are necessary to transport a given
quantity of CO2. The result is an increase in transportation costs.

6. Conclusion

The work examines the possibility of direct ocean release of CO2/N2 mixtures for CO2
sequestration. It develops a numeric model for the direct ocean release of CO2/N2
mixtures for CO2 sequestration built on past models and experiments of direct release of
pure CO2 . The numeric model is used to simulate ocean release of varying composition
mixtures at different injection depths. The simulation results are used to compare gas
mixture release to the P-GLAD system for low purity CO2 sequestration (Saito et al.,
2000) and the direct release of pure CO2.

The simulation results show that at depths below the hydrate zone low purity mixtures
exhibit faster dissolution rates due to the elimination of hydrate formation. At depths
above the hydrate formation zone, the initial bubble concentration has little effect on
bubble dissolution measured by percent of CO2 dissolved. It was found that all bubbles
injected above 500 m dissolve 95% or more of the injected CO2 before reaching the upper
mixed zone. It is important to note, however, that the absolute mass of CO2 dissolved
decreases with increasing impurity.

It was also shown that low purity bubbles accelerate as they rise due to the expansion of
the insoluble gas and the decrease in bubble density. Pure CO2 bubbles, on the other,
decelerate as their bubble diameter shrinks.

The comparison of reported mass transfer coefficients shows the discrepancy in
predictions that result from uncertainties in those coefficients. This suggests a need for
more experimental work and the development of more accurate mass transfer coefficient
correlations, both for pure and mixed gas bubbles.

                                                                                                                                82
Finally, this report makes an estimation of the costs of the direct release of a 15% CO2
bubble injected at 1000 m of 118$ per a ton-CO2 injected. This compares to estimated
costs of 68 $/ton-CO2 for the P-GLAD and 126$ per a ton-CO2 for the direct release of
liquid CO2.
