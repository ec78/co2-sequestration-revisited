# Literature Update (2002 → 2026)

Findings from the Phase 1 literature search (see [PROJECT_THESIS.md](PROJECT_THESIS.md)
Section 5, Phase 1). Purpose: check the informal claims made in
PROJECT_THESIS.md Section 2 against actual sources, and give Phase 2 (the
reframing decision) something concrete to work from. Searches run via
WebSearch/WebFetch; not a full systematic review — follow-up reading of the
primary sources below is still needed before anything here is cited directly
in a revised report.

## 1. Regulatory status: direct ocean CO2 disposal is not legal, and hasn't
   been since 2007 — **confirmed**

On 2 November 2006, Contracting Parties to the London Protocol amended
Annex 1 to explicitly permit CO2 stream disposal **only** into sub-seabed
geological formations, conditioned on the stream being "overwhelmingly CO2"
with no added waste. The amendment entered into force 10 February 2007.
Water-column disposal — the entire subject of the 2002 report, both the
CO2/N2 mixture release model and the P-GLAD comparator — is excluded.
Specific sub-seabed assessment guidelines followed in 2007.

Sources:
- [IMO — Carbon Capture and Sequestration overview](https://www.imo.org/en/OurWork/Environment/Pages/CCS-Default.aspx)
- [WRI — London Protocol Adopts Amendment Allowing for Sub-seabed Storage](http://pdf.wri.org/css_06_12_08_london_protocol.pdf)
- [EPA — London Convention and London Protocol](https://www.epa.gov/marine-protection-permitting/london-convention-and-london-protocol-international-treaties-prevent)
- [NOAA — CCS in Sub-Seabed Geological Formations](https://www.noaa.gov/carbon-capture-and-storage-in-sub-seabed-geological-formations)

**Implication**: any revised report cannot present direct ocean release
(mixture or P-GLAD-style) as an implementable option under current
international law. This is now a closed question, not an open one.

## 2. What happened to the field trials — **confirmed, with detail beyond
   what was assumed in Section 2**

- A large field experiment off Kauai, Hawaii was proposed by PICHTR
  (an international US/Japan/Norway/Canada/Australia consortium formed
  under a 1997 Kyoto COP-3 agreement); EPA opened public comment in March
  2002, extended it after volume of opposition, and PICHTR withdrew the
  permit application in June 2002 without stating a reason. Comments
  received were "unanimous in opposition."
  ([Nature news, 2002](https://www.nature.com/articles/417888b);
  [Environment Hawai'i](https://www.environment-hawaii.org/?p=3210))
- The project then attempted to relocate to a site off Bergen, Norway;
  the Norwegian government revoked the permit for policy reasons as well.
- Smaller-scale MBARI field experiments (Brewer, Peltzer et al.) continued
  after 2002 for a few more years under an international program (OACE —
  Ocean Abyssal Carbon Experiment, with NMRI and University of Bergen),
  producing papers through ~2005 on plume behavior, hydrate formation, and
  ecological effects (meiofauna impacts, 2004; controlled 4 km-depth plume
  creation/sensing, 2005). This is the direct successor work to the Brewer
  et al. (1999, 2000) sources already cited in the 2002 report.
  ([MBARI — Brewer lab publications](https://www.mbari.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Brewer-lab-publications-2021.pdf))
- Greenpeace and other environmental groups began actively opposing this
  research direction around 2003, which the 2022 National Academies report
  identifies as a significant factor in halting momentum.
- By the time of AR4 (2007), IPCC still discussed ocean storage as a
  category; by AR5 (2014) and AR6 (2022), it had dropped out of the
  mitigation literature entirely, per the IPCC's own special report
  framing (2005) that flagged "unknown biological impacts, high costs,
  impermanence, and public acceptance" as the core barriers.

**Net read**: the original thesis was written at almost exactly the
moment this whole research program stalled out — the Hawaii withdrawal is
the same month/year window as the report itself (2002). Field experimentation
had a brief tail (through ~2005) but no field program in this space appears
to be active today.

## 3. P-GLAD / GLAD system — technical work continued briefly, then also
   appears to have stopped

Saito et al. and collaborators published follow-up technical-optimization
papers through about 2004–2005 (bubble structure at local/global scale,
pumping characteristics, design-factor correlations for the GLAD system).
No evidence surfaced of continued GLAD/P-GLAD research after the mid-2000s
— consistent with the same regulatory/political wind-down affecting the
whole direct-ocean-release research program, P-GLAD included, despite it
being marketed as avoiding some of the worst objections (shallow injection,
no liquefaction).

Source: [OSTI — Correlations among the design factors of the CO2 ocean sequestration system, GLAD](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20582120)

## 4. Current CCS cost benchmarks — the economic comparator has moved,
   not just inflated

- Current sub-seabed/geological CO2 storage (transport + injection +
  monitoring) costs run roughly **$7–50/ton**, with a commonly cited base
  case around **$20–22.5/ton** for the storage leg alone.
  ([Thunder Said Energy](https://thundersaidenergy.com/downloads/co2-disposal-in-geologic-formations-the-economics/))
- DOE's stated target is **capture** costs under $40/ton by 2025 and under
  $30/ton by 2035 — capture, not storage, is now the dominant cost driver
  in mainstream CCS, the opposite of the 2002 report's framing where
  separation/capture was the thing being routed *around*.
  ([Belfer Center](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/carbon-capture-utilization-and-storage-technologies-and-costs-us-context))
- 45Q tax credit: up to **$85/ton** for geological storage, **$60/ton**
  for CO2-EOR — i.e., federal policy in the US now directly subsidizes the
  geological-storage pathway the 2002 report treated as outside its scope.
- **Inflation check** against the 2002 figures: $118/ton (2002, mixture
  release), $68/ton (2002, P-GLAD), $126/ton (2002, liquid release),
  converted to ~2024 dollars at roughly 1.8–1.9× CPI inflation, come out to
  **≈$215–225, ≈$124–130, ≈$230–240/ton respectively**. Current all-in
  geological CCS costs (capture + transport + storage) are frequently
  quoted well under $150/ton and trending down, with storage alone at
  $20–50/ton. Even before accounting for the legal ban, **geological
  storage now looks substantially cheaper than any of the three 2002 ocean-
  release options were, in real terms.**

*Caveat*: 2002 figures and current figures are not apples-to-apples
(different scope, different depth/location assumptions, wide reported
ranges) — this comparison is a directional sanity check for Phase 4, not a
citable number yet.

## 5. Ocean-based CDR (mCDR) — the field that grew where direct-release
   research stopped

- National Academies, *A Research Strategy for Ocean-based Carbon Dioxide
  Removal and Sequestration* (2022) — assesses six mCDR approaches
  (alkalinity enhancement, electrochemical approaches, biomass
  sinking/blue carbon, artificial upwelling/downwelling, and others),
  commissioned by ClimateWorks Foundation.
  ([NAP](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/pubs/29342/) —
  note: this URL/ID may correspond to a related or updated release; verify
  before citing)
- A **Marine CDR Standing Committee** was established in 2025 specifically
  to keep this assessment current, with a digital report series and public
  release activity as recently as September 2026 — this is a live,
  actively maintained research area, unlike direct-release disposal.
  ([National Academies — Marine CDR Standing Committee](https://www.nationalacademies.org/units/DELS-OSB-24-03))
- Directionally, mCDR is the **inverse** of the 2002 thesis: pulling CO2
  out of seawater/atmosphere rather than injecting flue-gas CO2 into it.
  But it inherits much of the same underlying ocean chemistry, gas
  transfer, and — for some electrochemical/bubble-mediated approaches —
  potentially similar bubble/gas-transport physics to what the 2002 model
  addresses.

## 6. Hydrate thermodynamics tooling — has moved past CSMHYD, still
   actively researched

- CSMHYD (Sloan, 1998, the tool cited in the 2002 report) was succeeded by
  **CSMGem**, benchmarked against other commercial tools (DBRHydrate,
  Multiflash, PVTsim).
- As recently as 2024, published work reports newer models outperforming
  CSMGem specifically for CO2 hydrate phase equilibrium prediction —
  meaning this is not a solved problem the 2002 report can simply defer
  to a single updated reference tool; the literature is still actively
  refining CO2(+N2) hydrate models.
  ([Frontiers in Energy Research, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2024.1378754/full))
- CO2+N2-specific mixed-hydrate phase equilibria have continued to be
  measured and modeled (e.g., via CPA-EoS + van der Waals–Platteeuw
  combinations) well past Sloan (1998).

## 7. CO2 bubble mass-transfer coefficients — the 2002 report's flagged
   problem is apparently **still not resolved**

The 2002 conclusion explicitly called out large discrepancies between
reported mass-transfer coefficients as a research gap. Current literature
(a 2025 review of bubble-column mass-transfer measurement/prediction
methods, and 2019 work specifically on multi-bubble CO2 dissolution in
seawater) still treats this as an open, actively studied problem — driven
by sensitivity to bubble-surface contamination state (clean/mobile vs.
contaminated/rigid surfaces), bubble size, Reynolds/Schmidt number, etc.
([MDPI *Fluids*, 2025 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/fluids10020029);
[MDPI *JMSE*, 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/7/12/457))

**Implication for Phase 3**: this is good news for keeping the modeling
work relevant even if the disposal application is dead — the core
mass-transfer uncertainty the report identified is a real, still-open
problem, independent of whether anyone ever injects CO2 into open water
again.

## 8. Seawater property toolbox — `SEAWATER` (Morgan, 1994) is superseded

The `SEAWATER` MATLAB toolbox used in the 2002 model has been superseded
by the **Gibbs-SeaWater (GSW) Oceanographic Toolbox**, implementing
**TEOS-10** (the 2010 international standard), maintained at
[github.com/TEOS-10](https://github.com/TEOS-10/GSW-Matlab). GSW ships for
MATLAB, Fortran, C, and PHP per the TEOS-10 site; a Python port (`gsw` on
PyPI, community-maintained) is understood to exist and should be verified
directly before Phase 3 committing to it as the Python seawater-properties
dependency.

## Bottom line for Phase 2 (reframing decision)

The evidence supports the roadmap's default assumption in
[PROJECT_THESIS.md](PROJECT_THESIS.md) Section 4.1:

- The original topic (direct ocean water-column CO2 release, mixture or
  P-GLAD-style) is **legally foreclosed since 2007** and was already
  politically dead within the same year the report was written.
- It's **also no longer economically competitive** even ignoring the legal
  point — geological CCS storage costs today undercut the inflation-
  adjusted 2002 figures for all three options compared in the original
  report.
- The **underlying physics work is not obsolete**: the mass-transfer
  coefficient uncertainty the report flagged is still an open research
  problem, hydrate thermodynamics for CO2/N2 mixtures is still being
  actively refined, and there's a live adjacent field (mCDR) that shares
  much of the same ocean-chemistry/gas-transfer machinery, just pointed
  in the opposite direction.

This points toward the **moderate reframe** option already defaulted to in
Section 4.1: keep and modernize the technical core (Ch. 2–4 physics,
rebuilt model), but rewrite the framing from "here's a viable disposal
method and its cost" to "here's why this research direction closed, what
of the underlying science holds up, and where the adjacent live field
(geological CCS cost reality, mCDR) actually is today." Final call is
still the user's — this is Phase 2.
